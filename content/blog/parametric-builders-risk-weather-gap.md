Yes, it can. Builder's risk insurance pays when weather damages the work. It doesn't pay when weather stops the work. Parametric insurance fills that gap by paying a set amount when a measured trigger is hit at the site, such as a temperature, wind speed or rainfall threshold, whether or not anything was damaged.

That was the case a panel made on Tuesday at IRMI's Construction Risk Conference in National Harbor, as reported by Business Insurance.

## The gap builder's risk leaves

Weather stops work long before it breaks anything. Rose Hall, founder of construction risk advisory firm RH Business Ventures, said a day below 40 degrees could cut poured concrete's strength by 50%, and winds above 20 mph can make some crane operations unsafe. Neither shows up as a builder's risk claim.

"Contractors are just absorbing (non-covered weather losses), mostly silently," Hall said.

The panel also pointed to the human cost. Heat-related worker fatalities have risen, and builders often feel pressure to keep working through extreme conditions to stay on schedule.

## Cover the days that stop your work

Parametric cover doesn't have to insure every storm. Gary Kaplan, former president of construction at AXA XL, put it this way: "The goal is not to cover every weather event. It is to cover the specific events that stop your specific operations at your specific location."

That means a policy can be shaped around the job:

- Cold, but only on concrete pour days.
- Wind, but only on crane days.
- Payouts that can be used for anything, such as overtime to recover a delay.

Chris Smith, a senior director at the Baldwin Group, said larger builders could use it across a portfolio, and captive owners could use a payout to fund the captive's deductible after a catastrophic event. "To me, this is schedule guardianship," he said.

## A better weather number for the bid

Contractors usually set weather allowances in bids from rough estimates. Hall noted that a 10-year average can hide large swings from one year to the next. Kaplan proposed a "zero-weather-day bid," built on site-specific weather estimates, that could give a bidder an edge.

## What it means for project requirements

If parametric cover starts showing up in project requirements, it becomes one more line to verify, with its own triggers, limits and named site. As with every coverage, the certificate is only the summary. The policy terms are what pay.
