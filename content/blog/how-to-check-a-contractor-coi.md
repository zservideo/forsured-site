Knowing how to read a certificate of insurance is mostly knowing what it can't prove. The certificate summarizes a contractor's coverage on one page. Your job is to hold that summary against the project requirements for this job, line by line, and then ask for the documents behind anything that matters.

## What do you need before you read a certificate of insurance?

Learning how to read a certificate of insurance starts with the contract, not the certificate. Pull the insurance section of the subcontract or the project's insurance requirements exhibit. A contractor COI can only pass or fail against something written down. For this project, note:

- Each required coverage: general liability, auto, workers' compensation and employer's liability, umbrella or excess, and anything trade-specific like professional or pollution liability
- The minimum limit for each, and whether the umbrella must follow form
- Required endorsements: additional insured for ongoing and completed operations, waiver of subrogation, primary and noncontributory
- Who must be named: owner, lender, GC and anyone else the contract lists
- How long completed operations coverage must last after the job, and whether the aggregate must apply per project

Requirements differ by owner, lender and contract, so a template from the last job is a guess, not a standard.

## What does each ACORD 25 field tell you?

The ACORD 25 is the standard liability certificate, published by [ACORD](https://www.acord.org/standards-architecture/acord-forms). The current edition is 2025/12 (the [form as posted by the New York Department of Financial Services](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/12/acord-25-2025-12-liability.pdf)). Here's how to read a certificate of insurance on that form, field by field.

Table: ACORD 25 fields and what to check against your project requirements
| ACORD 25 field | What it shows | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Date | When the certificate was issued | A certificate from last year proves nothing about today |
| Producer | The agent or broker who issued it | Who you'll ask for fixes; not proof of coverage |
| Insured | The contractor's legal name and address | Matches the entity on the contract, not a parent, DBA or sister company |
| Insurers A to F, NAIC # | The carriers behind each policy | Real, named carriers; the NAIC number lets you look them up |
| INSR LTR | Which insurer writes each line | Every required line has a carrier |
| ADDL INSD | Additional insured is claimed on that line | A claim only; ask for the endorsement form |
| SUBR WVD | Waiver of subrogation is claimed | Same: ask for the endorsement |
| Policy number, EFF, EXP | Each policy and its dates | In force for the whole job; note every expiration |
| Limits | Each occurrence, aggregates, products and completed operations | At or above every required limit |
| Gen'l aggregate applies per | Policy, project or location | Per project, if your contract requires it |
| Umbrella or excess | Limit, occurrence or claims-made | Meets the requirement; follow form if required |
| Workers' comp and E.L. | Statutory coverage and employer's liability limits | Covers the states where the work is |
| Description of operations | Free text, often the project and named parties | Names this project and your contract where required |
| Certificate holder | Who asked for the certificate | Your correct legal entity |
| Cancellation | How notice of cancellation works | Notice follows the policy, not the certificate |

Forsured reads the certificate and the endorsement forms attached to each contractor COI, checks them against the requirements set for that project, and flags every line that falls short.

## What doesn't a contractor COI tell you?

A contractor COI on the ACORD 25 doesn't create coverage. The form says it's issued as a matter of information only and confers no rights on the certificate holder, and that it doesn't amend, extend or alter the policies. That's why [a COI alone isn't enough](/blog/compliance-isnt-coverage/).

Three lines on the form are worth knowing by heart:

- **The additional insured note.** If the certificate holder is an additional insured, the policy must have additional insured provisions or be endorsed. A statement on the certificate doesn't replace the endorsement.
- **The limits note.** The 2025/12 edition says the limits shown include amounts requested by the certificate holder and may not reflect policy limits above those requested. It also says limits may have been reduced by paid claims. A certificate that shows your required limit may show only your required limit.
- **The cancellation box.** "Should any of the above described policies be cancelled before the expiration date thereof, notice will be delivered in accordance with the policy provisions." The certificate holder isn't promised a notice. See [how to find out when a policy is canceled](/blog/notice-of-cancellation-coi/).

## What are the most common gaps on a contractor COI?

The most common contractor COI gaps look fine on a quick read. Anyone who knows how to read a certificate of insurance checks the same five places every time.

1. **Umbrella or excess below the requirement.** Project requirements often ask for more than a trade contractor carries on its standard program.
2. **Additional insured that stops when the job does.** CG 20 10 covers ongoing operations. You need completed operations too, usually CG 20 37 or a blanket form that includes it. See [completed operations additional insured](/blog/completed-operations-additional-insured/).
3. **An aggregate shared across every job.** When the "applies per" box says policy, one bad claim elsewhere can empty it. The fix is a designated construction project aggregate endorsement, which [IRMI defines](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/designated-construction-project-general-aggregate-limit-endorsement). See [per project aggregate](/blog/per-project-aggregate-coi/).
4. **A named insured that doesn't match the contract.** The wrong entity on the certificate means you can't be sure the right one is covered.
5. **Missing waiver or primary wording.** A checked SUBR WVD box isn't a waiver. Ask for the [waiver of subrogation endorsement](/blog/waiver-of-subrogation-coi/) and the [primary and noncontributory endorsement](/blog/primary-and-noncontributory/).

## How do state rules change what you check on a certificate of insurance?

State rules change which certificate of insurance lines you can rely on. Workers' compensation thresholds, monopolistic workers' comp states, license insurance minimums and anti-indemnity statutes that limit additional insured requirements all vary. Check the [contractor COI requirements for your state](/coi-requirements/) before you write the project requirements for limits or endorsements on a job there.

## How do you get a gap on a contractor COI fixed?

You get a gap on a contractor COI fixed by asking the contractor's broker for one specific change. The review checklist from [ISO](https://amtrustfinancial.com/getmedia/35303f0c-4a43-449b-bcb0-0fd07c75b947/Reviewing-Certificates-of-Insurance.pdf) gives the same advice: put the deficiency in writing and get a corrected certificate before work starts. A short note works best:

> Hi, for the Main Street project our project requirements call for umbrella limits of $5 million each occurrence and additional insured coverage for completed operations. The certificate dated October 2 shows $2 million umbrella and CG 20 10 only. Can you send an updated certificate and the CG 20 37 or equivalent endorsement?

Specific asks get fixed in days. Vague ones ("please send an updated COI") come back with the same certificate. And a review is only as good as its date, so recheck at every renewal and before every expiration on the job.
