## What tools help insurance brokers respond to a COI deficiency notice?

The tools that help insurance brokers respond to a COI deficiency notice fall into four groups: your agency management system, which issues the corrected certificate; a certificate network, which keeps issued certificates current; an outsourced certificate team, which takes the volume; and the general contractor's own compliance tool, which is where the notice came from and where the fix has to land.

None of them fixes the most common problem on its own. That's because most notices aren't certificate problems.

## What does a COI deficiency notice usually ask for?

A COI deficiency notice usually asks for coverage, not paperwork: an additional insured endorsement, often both ongoing and [completed operations](/blog/completed-operations-additional-insured/), a waiver of subrogation, primary and noncontributory wording, or a higher limit. Certificates miss often. IA Magazine cited industry estimates that [45% to 55% of initial COI submissions](https://www.iamagazine.com/2026/02/28/magic-in-a-bottle-challenges-and-opportunities-with-certificates-of-insurance/) are rejected for errors or missing information.

So before you open any tool, sort the notice into one of two piles:

1. **The coverage is on the policy, and the certificate didn't show it.** The holder name is wrong, the endorsement wasn't attached, or the description of operations is missing the project. A corrected certificate fixes this, usually the same day.
2. **The coverage isn't on the policy.** The endorsement was never added, or the limit is below the subcontract. No certificate can fix this. It's a conversation with the client and the carrier first, and a [certificate isn't coverage](/blog/compliance-isnt-coverage/) on its own.

Most delay comes from mixing the two up: a corrected certificate goes back, the general contractor rejects it again, and the email thread grows. The fastest tools are the ones that show you the exact requirement and the project, so you know which pile you're in on the first read. ## How should an agency set up its COI deficiency workflow?

Set up your COI deficiency workflow so that every notice gets sorted, answered once and closed where the general contractor will see it. Insurance brokers who do this the same way every time spend less of the week on rework.

1. **Ask for the requirement, not just the notice.** Get the project's insurance requirements and the subcontract section the notice points to. If the GC's tool shows them, work from there.
2. **Sort it.** Is the coverage on the policy and missing from the certificate, or missing from the policy? Only the first is a corrected certificate.
3. **Fix the coverage first.** Add the endorsement or raise the limit with the carrier, then issue the corrected certificate with the endorsement attached from your agency management system.
4. **Answer where the notice came from.** Upload to the GC's tool or link rather than replying to the email, so the item clears.
5. **Template what repeats.** Save holder-specific endorsements and certificate templates for general contractors you see often, so the next COI deficiency notice from them takes minutes.

## Common questions

### What tools help insurance brokers respond to contractor COI deficiency notices?

Four kinds: your agency management system, which issues and reissues certificates to holders; a certificate network such as Certificial, which keeps issued certificates current; an outsourced certificate team such as ReSource Pro's Certs Center for volume; and the general contractor's own compliance tool, where the notice came from and where the fix needs to land.

### What is a COI deficiency notice?

A COI deficiency notice is a message from a general contractor, or their compliance tool, saying a contractor's certificate of insurance doesn't meet the project's requirements. It usually lists what's missing, such as an additional insured endorsement, a waiver of subrogation, primary and noncontributory wording, or a limit below what the subcontract requires.

### Can a broker fix a missing endorsement by reissuing the certificate?

Only when the coverage is already there and the certificate left it out, such as a missing holder name or an endorsement that's on the policy but wasn't attached. If the policy lacks the endorsement or the limit, the fix is a policy change first. A corrected certificate can't show coverage the policy doesn't have.

### Why do so many contractor certificates get rejected?

Industry estimates cited by IA Magazine put the rejection rate for initial COI submissions at 45% to 55%, for errors or missing information. The common causes are endorsements that weren't attached, limits below the subcontract, and requirements that differ by project, so a certificate that passed on one job fails on the next.

### Do insurance brokers have to join a general contractor's compliance platform?

Usually not. Many GC tools take uploads through a link with no account, and some offer brokers their own access. The Big I has cautioned agents about third-party platforms that ask for policy data in place of a certificate, so read what a platform asks you to submit before you sign up.

### Should an agency outsource certificate work?

It can make sense when certificate requests crowd out account work. ReSource Pro's Certs Center, for example, handles agency certificate work on a pay-per-use model. Keep coverage questions in house, though: an outsourced team can issue a corrected certificate, but deciding whether to add an endorsement is a conversation with the client and the carrier.
