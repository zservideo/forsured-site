What does "per written contract" mean on a blanket additional insured endorsement?

It means the policy adds whoever a written contract requires, without naming anyone. Whether "per written contract" covers you depends on the endorsement's wording, whether your contract is with the named insured, and whether it was signed before the loss. CG 20 33 and CG 20 38 answer the first question differently.

The short version

  • "Per written contract" means the policy adds as an additional insured whoever a written contract requires, instead of naming each party on a schedule.
  • CG 20 33 requires a contract between you and the named insured. CG 20 38 can reach parties further up the contract chain.
  • New York's highest court held in 2018 that "with whom you have agreed" by written contract requires that direct contract.
  • Courts also look at whether the agreement was in writing, signed before the loss, and actually required additional insured status.
  • A certificate doesn't create additional insured status. Get the endorsement and keep the signed contract on file.

"Per written contract" means the policy adds as an additional insured whoever a written contract requires it to add, instead of naming each party on a schedule. That's what makes an endorsement a blanket additional insured endorsement. It's convenient: one endorsement covers every owner and GC a contractor works for. But it only covers you if your project fits the endorsement's exact words, and those words differ more than most people expect.

As IA Magazine put it, "There is no such thing as a blanket additional insured endorsement." ISO offers several automatic forms, and they differ greatly. Insurers write their own versions too. The County of Sonoma's insurance guide notes that many blanket additional insured endorsements are insurer proprietary forms, not ISO forms, and that each one has to be read against the contract.

Whose contract counts under per written contract wording?

The biggest question is whether the endorsement covers only parties the named insured contracted with directly. Lawyers call that privity of contract.

New York's Court of Appeals answered it for one common wording in 2018. In Gilbane Building Co./TDX Construction Corp. v. St. Paul Fire and Marine Insurance Co., a construction manager on a Bellevue Hospital Campus project required each prime contractor to name it as an additional insured. One prime contractor's policy covered "any person or organization with whom you have agreed to add as an additional insured by written contract." The construction manager had no contract of its own with that prime contractor. The court ruled 5-2 that "with whom" meant the written contract had to be with the additional insured, so the construction manager wasn't covered, as Jones Day reported. The prime contract's insurance requirements couldn't rewrite the policy.

Other courts have read similar per written contract language both ways. IRMI reports that an Illinois court found "such person or organization" required a direct written agreement, while federal courts in Maine and Connecticut found similar wording didn't.

What's the difference between CG 20 33 and CG 20 38?

ISO's two construction blanket additional insured forms answer the privity question differently.

CG 20 33 and CG 20 38 compared, per IA Magazine and Jones Day

Who it addsParties the named insured has a written construction agreement withThose parties, plus other parties that agreement requires it to add
Privity of contractRequiredNot necessarily required
Covers an owner above the GCOnly with a direct contractYes, if the contract requires it

So when an owner's contract requires the GC to add the owner, and the GC's contracts pass that requirement down, CG 20 38 is the form built to carry the owner along. CG 20 33 covers only the GC that signed with the contractor. Whether either one reaches finished work is a separate question, so check that part of the wording on the contractor COI's endorsement too, and see completed operations additional insured coverage.

Does the contract have to be in writing and signed before the loss?

Per written contract wording can raise three more questions, and IRMI found courts split on each:

  • Is it in writing? Some courts have accepted letters, faxes and proposals. In Atofina v. Continental Casualty (2005), the Texas Supreme Court accepted a written construction proposal. Two New York appellate courts have said an oral agreement could count under some wording.
  • Was it signed in time? Many endorsements require the agreement to be executed before the loss, and IRMI says many courts are inflexible on that. In Empire Builders v. Delos (2010), coverage failed because the agreement came too late.
  • Does it actually require additional insured status? In A.F. Lusi Construction v. Peerless (2004), the Rhode Island Supreme Court found no additional insured agreement because the trade contract's required insurance section was blank. A certificate noting a blanket endorsement didn't fix it.

These fights are still live. In a lawsuit filed in federal court in New York and reported by Insurance Business in July 2026, Berkley alleges Trisura refused to defend a project owner under an endorsement whose schedule read "Per Written Contract." Part of the dispute, as reported, is whether a purchase order's insurance exhibit counts as a written contract. The allegations haven't been decided.

How do you check per written contract wording on a contractor COI?

You check per written contract wording by reading the endorsement, not the certificate:

  1. Get the endorsement. Jones Day notes that additional insured status is created only by an endorsement the insurer issues. Ask for the declarations and schedule of forms too.
  2. Find the form number and edition. CG 20 33, CG 20 38 or an insurer's own form each read differently.
  3. Read the wording. Look for "with whom," "such person or organization" and any execution or timing requirement.
  4. Check it reaches every party your project requirements name, including owners who don't contract with that contractor directly.
  5. Keep the signed contract, with its insurance requirements, on file next to the endorsement and the contractor COI.
  6. Recheck at renewal, when forms can change.

IA Magazine's advice to agents applies to you as the reader: a certificate should list the endorsement and its edition date, and you should get a copy. "Always read the COI like a person with no insurance background would."

What else should your project requirements say?

A blanket additional insured endorsement is only as good as the contract behind it. Write your project requirements so the contract does the work: name every party that needs additional insured status, require it for ongoing and completed operations, and require it to be primary and noncontributory. Sign the contract before work starts. Then check that your state's rules allow what you're asking for, using the contractor COI requirements for your state.

Common questions

What is a blanket additional insured endorsement?

A blanket additional insured endorsement grants additional insured status to any party a written contract requires, instead of listing each party by name. IA Magazine points out that ISO offers several of these automatic forms and they differ greatly, so two blanket endorsements on two contractor COIs can cover very different parties. Read the form number, edition and wording.

What's the difference between CG 20 33 and CG 20 38?

CG 20 33 adds a party automatically only when the named insured has a written construction agreement with that party, so it requires privity of contract. CG 20 38 also covers other parties the named insured's contract requires it to add, such as an owner above the GC. Jones Day suggests owners and GCs consider CG 20 38 for that reason.

Does a per written contract endorsement cover an owner with no direct contract with the insured?

It depends on the wording. In Gilbane v. St. Paul Fire and Marine (2018), New York's Court of Appeals held 5-2 that coverage for parties "with whom you have agreed" by written contract required a direct contract with the named insured. The construction manager had none, so it wasn't covered. Other wording, like CG 20 38, reaches further.

Does the contract have to be signed before the loss?

Often, yes. Many blanket endorsements cover parties only under a contract executed before the injury or damage happened, and IRMI reports that many courts are inflexible on that requirement. In Empire Builders v. Delos (2010), a New York court accepted that an oral agreement could count but denied coverage because it wasn't executed before the loss.

Is a certificate of insurance enough to prove additional insured status?

No. A certificate summarizes coverage and confers no rights. Additional insured status comes from an endorsement the insurer issues, and for per written contract wording, from a contract that meets the endorsement's terms. Ask for the endorsement, the schedule of forms and the declarations, as Jones Day advises, and keep them with the contractor COI.

Does a blanket endorsement increase the policy limits?

No. Additional insured endorsements don't increase the policy limits, as the County of Sonoma's insurance guide notes. The named insured and every additional insured share the same limits, so a contractor working several projects under one blanket additional insured endorsement is spreading one set of limits across all of them. Check the aggregate on the contractor COI.
